# The Septuagint

The Septuagint is the Greek translation of the Jewish scriptures, made by and for the Jews of the Greek-speaking world,
above all at Alexandria in Egypt. It was the Old Testament of the early Church, and it is where the Catholic Old
Testament's deuterocanonical books come from.

## The Letter of Aristeas

The name comes from a story. The *Letter of Aristeas* tells that King Ptolemy II Philadelphus wanted the Jewish Law for
his library at Alexandria, and that seventy-two Israelites, six from each tribe, translated it; hence *Septuaginta*,
"the seventy". Scholars regard the story as partly legendary, but its core holds: the Law, the five books of Moses, was
translated, probably at Alexandria, in the middle of the third century BC, and the other books followed, most of them in Greek
before about 130 BC.

## More books, and longer ones

The Greek Bible contains books the Hebrew Bible does not: Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch and the two books of
Maccabees, with the Greek parts of Esther and of Daniel (the song of the three young men in the furnace, Susanna, and
Bel and the dragon). The Catholic Church counts these seven books and the Greek parts as canonical, the deuterocanonical
books; the churches of the Reformation call them the Apocrypha. Its Old Testament has 46 books: 39 protocanonical and
7 deuterocanonical.

Scholars once spoke of an "Alexandrian canon" in Greek, wider than the Hebrew, which the Christians adopted. The
Pontifical Biblical Commission notes that recent research and discoveries have cast doubt on that view.

Some books differ inside, too. The Greek Jeremiah is shorter than the Hebrew and in a different order, and the Greek
Esther and Daniel are longer (see [why editions differ](/docs/why-editions-differ#greek-and-hebrew)). The Greek also
numbers most of the Psalms one lower than the Hebrew, between Psalm 9 and Psalm 147, because it joins and divides some
psalms differently; the Latin Bible inherited that numbering ([the Psalms' two
numberings](/docs/why-editions-differ#psalm-numbering)).

## The Bible of the early Church

The Second Vatican Council recalls that the Church "from the very beginning accepted as her own that very ancient Greek
translation of the Old Testament which is called the Septuagint" (*Dei Verbum*, 22). The great Christian codices,
[Sinaiticus](/docs/codex-sinaiticus), [Vaticanus](/docs/codex-vaticanus) and [Alexandrinus](/docs/codex-alexandrinus),
are among its chief manuscripts.

## Editions

Alfred Rahlfs' handy edition of 1935 rests mainly on those three codices (known as B, S and A). Robert Hanhart's revision,
the *Rahlfs–Hanhart* of 2006, corrected its text and apparatus at well over a thousand places. The full critical
edition was the work of the Göttingen Septuagint project, launched on 1 April 1908 with Rahlfs as its first director;
by its close in 2015 it had published 24 volumes, about two thirds of the corpus, and the work continues.
