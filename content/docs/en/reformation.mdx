# The Reformation and the Bible

A Catholic Bible has 73 books; most Protestant Bibles have 66. The difference is the seven deuterocanonical books of the
Old Testament and the Greek parts of Esther and Daniel ([the canon of Scripture](/docs/canon)), which the churches of
the Reformation call the "Apocrypha". The difference goes back to the sixteenth century, and to two questions the
Reformers answered differently from the Catholic Church: where the word of God is found, and how the Church knows which
books are Scripture.

## Scripture alone

The Reformers held that Scripture alone, *sola scriptura*, is the rule of faith. "The affirmation that by Scripture
alone (*sola scriptura*) one could gain access to all the truth that God desired to reveal to humanity made Martin
Luther the more scrupulous in defining the canon of sacred texts" (S. C. Karant-Nunn). The Lutheran *Formula of Concord*
(1577) confesses that "the only rule and norm, according to which all dogmas and all doctors ought to be esteemed and
judged, is no other whatever than the prophetic and apostolic writings both of the Old and of the New Testament". The
Reformed *Gallican Confession* (1559) adds that no authority "of antiquity, or custom, or numbers, or human wisdom, or
judgments, or proclamations, or edicts, or decrees, or councils" should be opposed to the Scriptures.

On how the canon is known, the Reformed confessions appeal to the Holy Spirit rather than to the Church. The *Gallican
Confession* knows the books to be canonical "not so much by the common accord and consent of the Church, as by the
testimony and inward illumination of the Holy Spirit"; the *Belgic Confession* (1561) receives them "not so much because
the Church receives and approves them as such, but more especially because the Holy Ghost witnesseth in our hearts that
they are from God". The Catholic Church teaches that Scripture and Tradition "form one sacred deposit of the word of
God", that "it is not from Sacred Scripture alone that the Church draws her certainty about everything which has been
revealed", and that it is through Tradition that "the Church's full canon of the sacred books is known" (*Dei Verbum*, 8–10).

## Luther and the Old Testament

The question of the canon came up in controversy. At Leipzig in 1519, when Johann Eck cited 2 Maccabees for the
doctrine of purgatory, Luther answered that the book had no binding authority, since it was outside the canon. In 1520
Andreas Karlstadt published the first treatise on the canon of the period, *De canonicis scripturis libellus*: he called
Wisdom, Sirach, Judith, Tobit and the books of Maccabees *apocryphi*, "outside the Hebrew canon", and yet holy writings.

In his complete German Bible of 1534 Luther kept these books, but set them apart, between the Old Testament and the
New, in a section with its own heading: *Apocrypha. Das sind Bücher, so nicht der heiligen Schrifft gleich gehalten,
vnd doch nützlich vnd gut zu lesen sind*, "Apocrypha: that is, books which are not held equal to holy Scripture, and
yet are useful and good to read". He translated Judith, Wisdom, Tobit, Sirach, Baruch, the two books of Maccabees and
the Greek parts of Esther and Daniel, and wrote prefaces to some of them. In this the Reformers followed Saint Jerome,
who had preferred the Hebrew canon (see [the canon of Scripture](/docs/canon#jerome-and-augustine)); it is from them
that the name "Apocrypha" came to mean these books. In Zurich, Leo Jud published a translation of the Apocrypha in 1529.

## Luther and the New Testament

Luther's New Testament of September 1522 numbered twenty-three books, and then printed four more at the end, without
numbers: Hebrews, James, Jude and Revelation. Compared with the Gospel and first letter of John, the letters of Paul
and the first letter of Peter, "the books that show you Christ", he called James *eyn rechte stroern Epistel*, "a right
strawy epistle". In his prefaces he praised James as "a good book", but would not have it "numbered among the true
chief books, though I would not thereby prevent anyone from including or extolling him as he pleases"; of Revelation
he wrote that he left "everyone free to hold his own opinions".

For more than a century Luther's followers held these four books apart; by 1700, Bossuet could say that Catholics and Protestants agreed on
the New Testament. The only trace left is the order of German Protestant Bibles, with Hebrews and James at the end. The
Anglicans and Calvinists always kept the whole New Testament (*Catholic Encyclopedia*).

## The Reformed confessions

The confessions of the Reformed churches list the 39 books of the Hebrew canon as the Old Testament, and the 27 books of
the New. They differ on what to make of the others:

- The **Gallican Confession** (1559) calls them "ecclesiastical books upon which, however useful, we can not found any
  articles of faith".
- The **Belgic Confession** (1561) names them, from Tobit to the two books of Maccabees, and says that the Church "may
  read and take instruction from" them, "so far as they agree with the canonical books", but may not confirm from them
  "any point of faith".
- The **Thirty-Nine Articles** of the Church of England (in English, 1571) quote Jerome: "And the other bookes, (as
  Hierome sayth) the Churche doth reade for example of lyfe and instruction of manners: but yet doth it not applie them
  to establishe any doctrene." The Church of England kept lessons from them in its liturgy.
- The **Westminster Confession** (1646), the most severe, says that "the books commonly called Apocrypha, not being of
  divine inspiration, are no part of the Canon of the Scripture", and are to be used no otherwise "than other human
  writings".

John Calvin, answering the Council of Trent in 1547, objected that admitting these books into the canon was "against the
consent of the primitive Church", though he granted that the Council of Carthage and Augustine had held the same view as
Trent. "I am not one of those, however, who would entirely disapprove the reading of those books."

## Trent's answer

The Council of Trent replied in its fourth session, on 8 April 1546, by defining the canon of 73 books, received "with
all their parts", and by receiving Scripture and the apostolic traditions "with an equal affection of piety, and
reverence" ([the canon of Scripture](/docs/canon#the-council-of-trent)). Calvin summed up what was at stake in four
points: that "in doctrine we are not to stand on Scripture alone, but also on things handed down by tradition"; that the
council placed the disputed books "in the same rank with the others"; that it made the Vulgate authentic; and that it
claimed the right to interpret Scripture.

## From the Apocrypha to 66 books

Until the nineteenth century Protestant Bibles still printed the Apocrypha, as Luther had, apart from the other books.
Then the change came: under the influence of the Presbyterians and Calvinists, the British and Foreign Bible Society
decided in 1826 not to distribute Bibles containing the Apocrypha, and since then their publication in Protestant Bibles
"has almost entirely ceased in English-speaking countries" (*Catholic Encyclopedia*, 1908). Lutheran Bibles in Germany
went on printing them, as an appendix.

## Today

Catholics and Bible Societies now translate the Bible together. The *Guidelines for Interconfessional Cooperation in
Translating the Bible*, agreed in 1968 and revised in 1987 by the Secretariat for Promoting Christian Unity and the
United Bible Societies, record that "it is the aim of the Bible Societies to provide the Scriptures in the canon desired
by the churches". A complete Bible "bearing the imprimatur of Roman Catholic authorities must contain the
deuterocanonical texts", while most Protestants want them clearly distinguished from the Hebrew canon; in a common
edition they are printed "as a separate section before the New Testament", with the complete Greek text of Esther in that
section and the Hebrew text among the books of the Hebrew canon. In Catholic Bibles, such as those on this site, they
stand in their places among the other books (see [why editions differ](/docs/why-editions-differ)).
