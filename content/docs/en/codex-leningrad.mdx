# The Leningrad Codex

The Leningrad Codex is the oldest complete dated manuscript of the Hebrew Bible. It was written in Egypt early in the
eleventh century, and it is kept today in the National Library of Russia in Saint Petersburg, which was called
Leningrad when the codex got its name. Because it is complete, it is the text that the standard scholarly editions of
the Hebrew Bible print.

## One scribe, five dates

The codex was the work of one man, Samuel ben Jacob. His colophon says that "this codex of the complete Bible was
written, furnished with vocalisation and masora, and carefully checked" in Medinat Miṣrayim, and the illuminated pages at
its end repeat that "Samuel ben Jacob wrote, vocalised and furnished with masora this codex": he wrote the consonants, the
vowels, the accents and the Masoretic notes, and the decorative micrography too. He made it for a patron, Mevorakh ben
Joseph, a Karaite.

The colophon dates the work in five eras: from the Creation, from the exile of King Jehoiachin, by the Seleucid era, from
the destruction of the Second Temple and from the Hijra. The five dates do not agree exactly; they run from 1008 to 1011,
and most of them fall in 1008–1009. So the codex is dated to 1008 or 1009 by most scholars; the National Library of Russia
gives 1008–1010. *Medinat Miṣrayim* is usually translated Cairo; Ben Outhwaite, of Cambridge University Library, has
argued that it means Fustat, the old city beside it.

## The book

The codex has 491 parchment leaves. Sixteen pages at its end are richly decorated "carpet pages", in which the lines of
the final Masoretic notes, in tiny script, form geometric patterns filled with coloured plant ornament, some of them
gilded. The Ben-Zvi Institute counts it among the manuscripts closest to the [Aleppo Codex](/docs/codex-aleppo): its
vowels and accents are very similar, and scholars have used it to reconstruct what the Aleppo Codex has lost.

## To Saint Petersburg

The codex came to Russia with the first collection of the Karaite scholar Abraham Firkovich (1787–1874). In 1862, by
order of Alexander II, 125,000 roubles were granted to buy the collection for the Imperial Public Library, and the
manuscripts arrived the following year. Its shelfmark is Firkovich Hebrew I B 19a (Евр. I B 19a).

## The text of the scholarly editions

Because it is complete, the codex became the base of the modern critical editions of the [Hebrew text](/docs/hebrew-text).
For the third edition of Rudolf Kittel's *Biblia Hebraica*, Paul Kahle arranged for the manuscript to be lent to the
University of Leipzig, and in 1937 the *Biblia Hebraica* appeared printing the text of the Leningrad Codex instead of the
received text, the *textus receptus*. Its successor, the *Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia* (1967–1977, edited by
Karl Elliger and Wilhelm Rudolph), is the only complete scholarly edition of the codex. The *Biblia Hebraica Quinta*,
appearing since 2004, presents its text and Masoretic notes again, from new colour photographs.

A photographic facsimile, *The Leningrad Codex: A Facsimile Edition*, edited by David Noel Freedman, was published in 1998.
The National Library of Israel's Ktiv digital library offers it in digitized microfilm.
