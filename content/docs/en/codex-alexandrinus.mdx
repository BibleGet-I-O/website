# Codex Alexandrinus

Codex Alexandrinus is a Greek Bible of the fifth century, copied in the eastern Mediterranean and now in the British
Library in London. It is the third of the great Greek codices, with [Sinaiticus](/docs/codex-sinaiticus) and
[Vaticanus](/docs/codex-vaticanus), both of the fourth century.

## Four volumes

The British Library keeps it in four volumes, as Royal MS 1 D V–VIII:

- **1 D V**: Genesis to 2 Chronicles;
- **1 D VI**: the Prophets and the books of the Maccabees, Hosea to 4 Maccabees;
- **1 D VII**: Athanasius' letter to Marcellinus on the Psalms, then the Psalms to Sirach;
- **1 D VIII**: the New Testament.

Its New Testament has lost the beginning of Matthew, up to 25:6, and two leaves of John (6:50–8:52). After the New
Testament it has the *First Letter of Clement* and part of the so-called *Second Letter of Clement*. The table of contents also lists the *Psalms
of Solomon*, now lost.

## From Alexandria to London

The codex takes its name from Alexandria. Athanasius III, patriarch of Alexandria, brought it there early in the
fourteenth century. Cyril Lucaris, patriarch of Alexandria and then of Constantinople, took it to Constantinople in 1621
and in 1625 offered it to the English ambassador, Sir Thomas Roe, as a gift for King James I. James died that year, and
it was Charles I who received it, in 1627. George II gave it to the British Museum in 1757 with the Old Royal Library.

## Why it matters

With Sinaiticus and Vaticanus it is one of the three manuscripts (A, S and B) on which Rahlfs' edition of the
[Septuagint](/docs/septuagint) mainly rests, and it is an important witness of the New Testament (see [the New
Testament's text](/docs/new-testament-text)).
