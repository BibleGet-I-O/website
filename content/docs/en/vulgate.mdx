# The Vulgate

The Vulgate is the Latin Bible of the Western Church: in large part Saint Jerome's translation, made at the end of the
fourth century. For more than a thousand years it was the Bible the Latin Church read, prayed and commented on, and two
editions on this site come from it: the [Clementine Vulgate](/read/vgcl) and, through translation, the
[Douay–Rheims](/read/drb). The Nova Vulgata, the Church's revision of it, is a third.

## Before Jerome: the Old Latin

Before Jerome the Latin Church read translations made from the Greek, the Old Testament from the
[Septuagint](/docs/septuagint). Scholars call them the Old Latin, or *Vetus Latina*.

## Jerome's work

Jerome came to Rome in 382, and Pope Damasus encouraged him to make a new Latin translation of the Bible. He began with
the Gospels, revising the Latin against the Greek (384). In 386 he settled in Bethlehem, and between 390 and 405 he
translated the Old Testament from the Hebrew. For the deuterocanonical books, which he did not find in the Hebrew, he
was generally content to correct the Old Latin. He died at Bethlehem in 419 or 420.

The Vulgate, the "common" Latin Bible, is the result: Jerome's translations, with parts of the older Latin. Jerome
translated Esther from the Hebrew and placed the Greek additions after it, at the end of the book, which is why the
Vulgate's Esther runs on to chapter 16 (see [why editions differ](/docs/why-editions-differ#esther)).

## The Council of Trent

On 8 April 1546, in its fourth session, the Council of Trent issued two decrees: one listing the books of the canon, and
one on the edition and use of the sacred books, which declared that "the old Latin Vulgate edition, which, in use for so
many hundred years, has been approved by the Church", be held as authentic in public lectures, disputations, sermons and
expositions. Pius XII explained in 1943 that this authenticity "is not specified primarily as critical, but rather as
juridical" (*Divino afflante Spiritu*, 21).

## The Sixtine and the Clementine

Pope Sixtus V published an edition of the Vulgate in 1590. It was hastily superseded: in 1592 Clement VIII issued the
authentic edition, the **Clementine Vulgate**. This is the text of the [Clementine Vulgate on this
site](/read/vgcl).

## Critical editions

Modern scholars have also reconstructed Jerome's own text from the oldest manuscripts. The handiest critical edition is
the Stuttgart *Biblia Sacra iuxta Vulgatam versionem*, edited by Robert Weber and Roger Gryson, first published in 1969;
its fifth edition appeared in 2007.

## The Nova Vulgata

After the Second Vatican Council, Paul VI set up a commission, on 29 November 1965, to revise the Latin Bible against the
Hebrew and Greek texts, for the liturgy. Its books appeared from 1969 to 1977, and John Paul II promulgated the whole as
the typical edition by the apostolic constitution *Scripturarum thesaurus* of 25 April 1979; a second typical edition
followed in 1986. For the books it revised it starts from the Stuttgart text.

In 2001 the instruction *Liturgiam authenticam* made the Nova Vulgata "the point of reference as regards the
delineation of the canonical text": in the deuterocanonical books, and wherever the manuscript traditions differ,
liturgical translations are to follow the same tradition as the Nova Vulgata (n. 37). Translations are still to be made
from the original texts; the Nova Vulgata is to be consulted alongside them, "as an auxiliary tool" (n. 24). The [Nova
Vulgata on this site](/read/nvbse) is the text the Holy See publishes.
