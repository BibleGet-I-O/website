# Codex Sinaiticus

Codex Sinaiticus is a Greek Bible of the fourth century, written by hand on parchment. With [Codex
Vaticanus](/docs/codex-vaticanus) it is one of the great Christian Bibles of that century, and it has the whole New
Testament.

## What it contains

Just over 400 large leaves survive. They hold around half of the Old Testament in Greek (the [Septuagint](/docs/septuagint),
with some of the deuterocanonical books), the whole New Testament, and two early Christian writings: the *Epistle of Barnabas* and part of the *Shepherd* of Hermas. In its New Testament the letters
of Paul, with Hebrews after 2 Thessalonians, come before Acts, which is followed by the Catholic letters and Revelation.

It is dated, from its handwriting, to the fourth century, sometimes more precisely to the middle of it; the British
Library dates it between the second and the third quarter of the century.

## From Sinai to four libraries

The codex is named after Saint Catherine's Monastery at the foot of Mount Sinai, where it was kept for centuries. In
1844 the German scholar Constantin von Tischendorf took 43 of its leaves to Leipzig. On a later visit, in 1859, he
obtained the bulk of the manuscript, which was presented to Tsar Alexander II of Russia, the transfer being completed in
1869. In December 1933 the British Museum bought those leaves from the Soviet government.

So the codex is divided today among four institutions:

- the **British Library**, London (Add MS 43725): 347 leaves, the largest part, Old Testament and the whole New Testament;
- the **Leipzig University Library**: the 43 leaves of 1844 (the *Codex Friderico-Augustanus*);
- the **National Library of Russia**, Saint Petersburg: fragments;
- **Saint Catherine's Monastery**, Sinai: leaves and fragments found there in 1975 (the British Library counts eighteen
  folios and fragments; Leipzig, twelve leaves and forty fragments).

## The digital codex

From 2005 to 2010 the four holders joined in the International Codex Sinaiticus Project, which reunited the
surviving pages online, at [codexsinaiticus.org](https://codexsinaiticus.org/), where
anyone can turn its pages.
