# The Qumran scrolls

The Dead Sea Scrolls are the oldest manuscripts of the Hebrew Bible we have, a thousand years older than the [Masoretic
codices](/docs/hebrew-text). They show, among much else, that before the Masoretes more than one form of some books was in
circulation.

## The discovery

In 1947 Bedouin of the Ta'amra tribe found seven scrolls by chance in a cave near Qumran, by the Dead Sea. From 1949
to 1956 further searches, among them the excavations of the École Biblique and the Rockefeller Museum led by Father Roland de Vaux, found ten more caves; the last, Cave 11, was discovered in 1956. Cave 4 alone
yielded some 15,000 fragments.

## What the caves held

The eleven caves held the remains of more than nine hundred different manuscripts (the Israel Antiquities Authority
counts over 900; the Israel Museum, some 950), most of them in fragments. About 230 of them are biblical scrolls; the others are
writings of other kinds. The scrolls were copied between the third century BC and the first century AD.

## The Great Isaiah Scroll

The best known is the Great Isaiah Scroll (1QIsa<sup>a</sup>), one of the seven found in 1947 and the only biblical
scroll that is almost complete: its 54 columns, on a parchment more than seven metres long, contain all 66 chapters of
Isaiah. It was copied in the second half of the second century BC (the Israel Museum gives about 125 BC, and also the
first century BC). It has been on display, with the other scrolls of Cave 1, in the Shrine of the Book at the Israel
Museum since 1965.

## More than one Hebrew text

Other scrolls show that some books circulated in more than one form. Jeremiah is the clearest case. The Greek
translation of Jeremiah, in the [Septuagint](/docs/septuagint), is shorter than the Hebrew and arranges the book
differently. Two Hebrew fragments from Cave 4 (4QJer<sup>b</sup> and 4QJer<sup>d</sup>) have the shorter form, while
others (2QJer, 4QJer<sup>a</sup>, 4QJer<sup>c</sup>) have the longer one. Most scholars conclude that the Greek
translators worked from a shorter Hebrew text, not that they abridged the book. See [why editions
differ](/docs/why-editions-differ#greek-and-hebrew).

## Hebrew Sirach

Sirach was written in Hebrew, but for centuries it was known only in its Greek translation, the one in the Catholic
Bible. In 1896 Agnes Smith Lewis and Margaret Dunlop Gibson brought leaves from the Cairo Genizah, the storeroom of an
old synagogue, to Cambridge, where Solomon Schechter recognised among them the Hebrew Ben Sira. Fragments of six
manuscripts have since been identified there, and more than half of the Hebrew book has been recovered. Older witnesses
came from Qumran and from Masada, whose scroll of Sirach, with chapters 39 to 44, Yigael Yadin published in 1965.
