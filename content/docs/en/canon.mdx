# The canon of Scripture

The word *canon* comes from the Greek *kanōn*, "rule". It means "the list of books which are accepted as inspired by God
and having a regulatory function for faith and morals" (Pontifical Biblical Commission, 2001). The Catholic canon has
73 books: 46 in the Old Testament and 27 in the New.

The Church did not make these books Scripture by listing them. The First Vatican Council taught that she holds them as
sacred and canonical "not because she subsequently approved them by her authority after they had been composed by
unaided human skill, nor simply because they contain revelation without error, but because, being written under the
inspiration of the holy Spirit, they have God as their author, and were as such committed to the church". What the
Church did was to recognize them: "It was by the apostolic Tradition that the Church discerned which writings are to be
included in the list of the sacred books" (*Catechism*, 120; see *Dei Verbum*, 8). That recognition took centuries.

## The Old Testament

At the birth of Christianity, the Pontifical Biblical Commission writes, "closed collections of the Law and the
Prophets existed", but the collection of the "Writings" "was not as well defined either in Palestine or in the Jewish
diaspora". Towards the end of the first century about 24 books (often counted as 22) were generally accepted by Jews as
sacred, "but it is only much later that the list became exclusive"; the Jewish canon "was not definitively fixed before
the end of the second century". The so-called Council of Jamnia, often said to have fixed it, "was more in the nature of
a school or an academy", and "there is no evidence of a decision drawing up a list of books".

The first Christians read their Scriptures in Greek, in the [Septuagint](/docs/septuagint), and the New Testament shows
that "a sacred literature wider than the Hebrew canon circulated in Christian communities". What the Church received
was "a body of Sacred Scripture which, within Judaism, was in the process of becoming canonical"; and "it was not the
Alexandrian Jews who fixed the exclusive canon of Scripture, but the Church, beginning from the Septuagint". The older
idea of two Jewish canons, a shorter one in Hebrew and a longer "Alexandrian" one in Greek, is now doubted.

The early Church's Old Testament "took different shapes in different regions". Most Christian writings of the second
century, and the Bible manuscripts from the fourth century on, use or contain books "which were not admitted into the
Hebrew canon". In the East, from Origen on, some tried to conform Christian usage to the Hebrew canon, without a lasting
effect.

## Jerome and Augustine

Saint Jerome, who translated the Old Testament from the Hebrew (see [the Vulgate](/docs/vulgate)), held to the Hebrew
canon. In his preface to the books of Samuel and Kings he wrote that Wisdom, Sirach, Judith, Tobit and the *Shepherd*
"are not in the canon", and in his preface to the books of Solomon: "As, then, the Church reads Judith, Tobit, and the
books of Maccabees, but does not admit them among the canonical Scriptures, so let it read these two volumes for the
edification of the people, not to give authority to doctrines of the Church." Even so he translated Tobit and Judith,
and for the other books not in the Hebrew he was content to correct the Old Latin.

Saint Augustine took the opposite view, and based it on the practice of the Church. In the canonical Scriptures, he
wrote, the reader "must follow the judgment of the greater number of Catholic churches". His list of the Old Testament
has "forty-four books", Tobit, Judith, the two books of Maccabees, Wisdom and Sirach among them, and his New Testament has
the 27 books. At the beginning of the fifth century, the Commission writes, regional councils "adopted his position in
drawing up the Old Testament canon", and "the unanimity expressed in their lists represents Church usage in the West".

## The New Testament

The letters of the apostles were first read in the churches they were written to, passed on to others, kept, and
"eventually accepted as Scripture"; "in this way, the canon of the New Testament was gradually formed within the
apostolic Tradition" (Pontifical Biblical Commission).

- The **Muratorian fragment**, a list of the New Testament books found in Milan in the eighteenth century, is usually
  dated to the late second century (some scholars argue for the fourth). It is silent on Hebrews, James and 2 Peter, and
  it names the Apocalypse of Peter, "with the remark that some will not allow" it to be read in church.
- **Eusebius of Caesarea**, about 325, sorted the books into those accepted by all; the disputed ones, "which are
  nevertheless recognized by many" (James, Jude, 2 Peter, 2 and 3 John); and the rejected.
- **Saint Athanasius**, in his Easter letter of 367, gave "the first extant list of precisely the twenty-seven books of
  the current New Testament canon": "Let no man add to these, neither let him take ought from these." His Old Testament
  is not the Catholic one: he counts Baruch and the Letter of Jeremiah with Jeremiah, but places Wisdom, Sirach, Esther,
  Judith and Tobit among the books "not indeed included in the Canon", to be read by those who are learning the faith.

## Councils and popes

- **Rome, 382.** A list of the books, the so-called *Decretum Gelasianum*, is traditionally attributed to a council
  held under Pope Damasus in 382. Since the study of Ernst von Dobschütz (1912), most scholars date the document as we
  have it to the sixth century, as a private compilation.
- **Hippo (393) and Carthage (397, 419).** The African councils, in which Augustine was the leading spirit, "drew up
  identical lists from which no sacred books are excluded" (*Catholic Encyclopedia*). The council of 419 confirmed them:
  its list includes Tobit, Judith, two books of Maccabees, "the five books of Solomon" (Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, the Song
  of Songs, Wisdom and Sirach) and fourteen letters of Paul, "for these are the things which we have received from our
  fathers to be read in church".
- **Pope Innocent I, 405.** Asked by Exsuperius, bishop of Toulouse, the pope sent him a list of the sacred books, with
  all the deuterocanonical books.
- **Florence, 1442.** In its bull of union with the Copts, the Council of Florence professed "that one and the same God
  is the author of the old and the new Testament", and listed their books, Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Ecclesiasticus, Baruch
  and the two books of Maccabees among them. It listed them as inspired, without yet calling them "canonical".

## The Council of Trent

When the Reformers questioned the books outside the Hebrew canon ([the Reformation and the Bible](/docs/reformation)),
the Council of Trent, among its first acts, defined the canon. In its fourth session, on 8 April 1546, it inserted a
list of the sacred books "lest a doubt may arise in any one's mind, which are the books that are received by this
Synod": the 46 books of the Old Testament and the 27 of the New. It received them, "seeing that one God is the author of
both", together with the apostolic traditions, "with an equal affection of piety, and reverence". And it declared:

> But if any one receive not, as sacred and canonical, the said books entire with all their parts, as they have been
> used to be read in the Catholic Church, and as they are contained in the old Latin vulgate edition; and knowingly and
> deliberately contemn the traditions aforesaid; let him be anathema.

"With all their parts" covers the passages once disputed, such as the end of Mark (16:9–20), the bloody sweat in Luke
(22:43–44) and the woman taken in adultery in John (7:53–8:11). The council did not decide anything new: it based its
decision "on immemorial tradition as manifested in the decrees of previous councils and popes, and liturgical reading"
(*Catholic Encyclopedia*). In the same session it declared the Vulgate "authentic" (see [the Vulgate](/docs/vulgate)).

The First Vatican Council (1870) confirmed Trent's list and taught why the books are canonical; the Second Vatican
Council taught that the books of both Testaments, "in their entirety, with all their parts, are sacred and canonical
because written under the inspiration of the Holy Spirit" (*Dei Verbum*, 11). The *Catechism* lists them (120): 46 books
of the Old Testament ("45 if we count Jeremiah and Lamentations as one") and 27 of the New.

## Protocanonical and deuterocanonical

In 1566 the Dominican Sixtus of Siena distinguished the **protocanonical** books, "always received by Christendom
without dispute", from the **deuterocanonical**, "whose Scriptural character was contested in some quarters". The
distinction "does not indicate a difference of authority, but only a difference of time at which the books were
recognized by the whole Church as Divinely inspired" (*Catholic Encyclopedia*).

The deuterocanonical books of the Old Testament are seven: Tobit, Judith, 1 and 2 Maccabees, Wisdom, Sirach and Baruch
(with the Letter of Jeremiah), with the Greek parts of Esther and of Daniel (3:24–90, and chapters 13 and 14); they were
"accepted only after centuries of hesitation" (Pontifical Biblical Commission). The New Testament has its own: Hebrews,
James, 2 Peter, 2 and 3 John, Jude and Revelation, whose place was once questioned in some churches.

The churches of the Reformation call the deuterocanonical books of the Old Testament the "Apocrypha". For Catholics,
"apocrypha" are the books "which were never received into the canon", which Protestants call the "pseudepigrapha"; the
same word means two different things (Secretariat for Promoting Christian Unity and United Bible Societies, 1987).
