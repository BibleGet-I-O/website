# The Hebrew text

The books of the Hebrew Bible were written in Hebrew, with a few chapters in Aramaic (some books of the Catholic Old
Testament, such as Wisdom, come to us in Greek), and the Church asks that translations be made
"especially from the original texts" (*Dei Verbum*, 22). This page tells how that text was arranged, copied and fixed, and which
manuscripts and editions scholars use today.

## Three parts, twenty-four books

The Jewish scriptures fall into three parts: the Law (*Torah*), the Prophets (*Nevi'im*) and the Writings (*Ketuvim*).
The division is old: it is already known to the prologue of Sirach, in the second century BC. Jews count twenty-four
books, a number often reduced to twenty-two; to them correspond the thirty-nine protocanonical books of the Christian Old
Testament, which counts as separate books what the Jewish tradition joins, such as the twelve minor prophets.

## Scribes and Masoretes

The books were copied by hand, letter by letter. Early Hebrew writing had no vowels: a word was written with its
consonants, and readers supplied the rest from a living tradition of reading aloud. Verse divisions, too, go back to the
early scribes; the Talmud already speaks of them.

The Masoretes, the scholars of the *masora* ("tradition"), set that reading down in writing. They added signs for the
vowels and for the chanting (the accents), and filled the margins with notes that count words and letters and record
unusual forms, so that later copyists would change nothing. The vowel signs were not yet used in Saint Jerome's time;
they were certainly complete in the ninth century. Of the systems devised, the one of the school of Tiberias, in
Galilee, prevailed, and the text it fixed is called the Masoretic text.

## The Aleppo Codex and the Leningrad Codex

The two most important Masoretic manuscripts are Bibles in book form, or codices.

The **[Aleppo Codex](/docs/codex-aleppo)** was written at Tiberias around 930 by the scribe Shlomo ben Buya'a, and
Aharon ben Asher, the most renowned of the Masoretes, added its vowels, accents and notes; Maimonides took it as his
model. Kept for six centuries at Aleppo, it lost about two fifths of its leaves in 1947, almost all of the Pentateuch
among them, and is now in the Shrine of the Book at the Israel Museum in Jerusalem.

The **[Leningrad Codex](/docs/codex-leningrad)** was written in Egypt by the scribe Samuel ben Jacob in 1008 or 1009 (its
colophon's five dates do not quite agree). It is the oldest complete dated manuscript of the Hebrew Bible, and is kept
at the National Library of Russia in Saint Petersburg (Firkovich, Hebrew I B 19a).

## Printed editions and critical editions

The first complete printed Hebrew Bible came out at Soncino, near Cremona, on 22 April 1488, printed by Yehoshua
Shelomoh of the Soncino family with Abraham ben Ḥayyim dei Tintori, after editions of the Former Prophets (1485) and the
Latter Prophets (1486); it gave the whole Bible with its vowels and accents. The Bibles that Daniel Bomberg printed in
Venice from 1518 were the first Hebrew Bibles to carry the chapter
divisions of the Latin Bible; his great Bible of 1547–48 numbered every fifth verse. Today scholars read the Hebrew text in critical editions, which print one
manuscript and record in an apparatus how other witnesses differ: the readings of other Hebrew manuscripts, of the Dead
Sea Scrolls, and of the ancient translations.

The standard edition is the *Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia* (BHS), edited by Karl Elliger and Wilhelm Rudolph and
published from 1967 to 1977; it is the only complete scholarly edition of the Leningrad Codex. Its successor, the
*Biblia Hebraica Quinta* (BHQ), also based on the Leningrad Codex, is appearing in twenty volumes, about half of them
published so far.

## The Hebrew text and the translations

Modern translations of the books of the Hebrew Bible are made from this text. Where the Greek Bible preserves a different Hebrew text, as in Jeremiah, the
[Qumran scrolls](/docs/qumran) can show which reading is older. The Hebrew text's own chapter and verse numbering is the
one most of these editions follow (see [why editions differ](/docs/why-editions-differ#chapters-and-verses-numbered-differently)).
