# Why editions differ

Open the same passage in two editions on this site and the two will not always match. A verse is numbered differently, a
psalm has another number, a book is longer in one Bible than in the other, or one column has a gap where the other has
text. None of these is a mistake: each edition follows a tradition, and the traditions go back to the manuscripts the
translators worked from. This page explains what you see in the reader, and why. The pages on [the Hebrew
text](/docs/hebrew-text), [the Septuagint](/docs/septuagint) and [the Vulgate](/docs/vulgate) tell the longer story.

## Chapters and verses numbered differently

The Bible's books were not written in chapters and numbered verses. The chapters used today go back to Stephen Langton,
archbishop of Canterbury, early in the thirteenth century; the verse numbers to the printer Robert Estienne, in his
Greek New Testament of 1551 and in his Latin Bible (sources disagree on its year: 1545, 1548 or 1555) (the Hebrew text's division into verses is older, the work of its
scribes and the Masoretes). The Hebrew Bible and the Latin Vulgate did not always place the divisions at the same points,
so the same words can be at the end of one chapter in one edition and at the start of the next in another.

The editions on this site follow one of two numberings. NABRE, CEI 2008, the Nova Vulgata and *El Libro del Pueblo de
Dios* follow the Hebrew text's chapters and verses; the Clementine Vulgate and the Douay–Rheims follow the Vulgate's. The
Riveduta usually agrees with the Vulgate, though not always. For example, the last verse of Genesis 31 in the Vulgate
(Laban's farewell) is <ReaderLink edition="NABRE" book={1} chapter={32} verse={1} with={["VGCL"]}>Genesis 32:1 in
NABRE</ReaderLink>; Malachi has three chapters in the Hebrew numbering and four in the Vulgate's, whose chapter 4 is the
Hebrew Malachi 3:19–24; and Joel has four chapters in one and three in the other.

The comparison lines verses up by their numbers. Where the numbering differs, a verse appears beside a neighbour of
another number, or beside a gap.

<H2 id="psalm-numbering">The Psalms' two numberings</H2>

The Psalter has 150 psalms in every Bible, but from Psalm 9 to Psalm 147 most of them have two numbers. The Greek
translation, the Septuagint, joins two Hebrew psalms into one in two places (9 and 10; 114 and 115) and splits two others
(116 and 147). The Latin Vulgate follows the Greek. So the psalm that begins "The Lord is my shepherd" is Psalm 23 in the
Hebrew numbering and Psalm 22 in the Greek and Latin.

On this site the Clementine Vulgate and the Douay–Rheims keep the Greek and Latin numbering; NABRE, CEI 2008, the Nova
Vulgata, *El Libro del Pueblo de Dios* and the Riveduta use the Hebrew one. The Nova Vulgata prints both, the Hebrew
first: *Psalmus 23 (22)*. Liturgical books differ again: the Italian lectionary, for instance, gives the Latin number
first. Where an edition's psalm has another number in the Hebrew count, the reader shows that number beside the title,
as with <ReaderLink edition="VGCL" book={23} chapter={22}>Psalm 22 of the Clementine Vulgate</ReaderLink>, which is
Psalm 23 in the Hebrew numbering. Compare <ReaderLink edition="VGCL" book={23} chapter={9} with={["NABRE"]}>the
Vulgate's Psalm 9 with NABRE</ReaderLink> to see one psalm of the Vulgate become two of the Hebrew.

<H2 id="greek-and-hebrew">Books with a Greek and a Hebrew form</H2>

Some books reached the Church in two forms: a Hebrew one, and a Greek one that is longer or arranged differently. The
Catholic Bible keeps the Greek material that the Hebrew lacks, and Catholic editions print it in different ways.

<H3 id="esther">Esther</H3>

The Hebrew Book of Esther has ten chapters. The Greek has 107 more verses: Mordecai's dream, the texts of the royal
decrees, and the prayers of Mordecai and of Esther, among them. Modern Catholic editions (NABRE, CEI 2008, the Nova
Vulgata, *El Libro del Pueblo de Dios*) print the Greek additions in their places, lettered (NABRE's sections A to F,
or verses such as 1:1a–1r), and give the Hebrew text as well. The reader shows the two side by side, as in
<ReaderLink edition="NABRE" book={19} chapter={4}>Esther 4 in NABRE</ReaderLink>, where Mordecai's and Esther's prayers
stand in the Greek column. The Clementine Vulgate and the Douay–Rheims follow Jerome, who translated the Hebrew and
placed the Greek additions after it, at the end of the book: from 10:4 to the end, chapters 11 to 16. The Riveduta, a Protestant Bible, has
only the Hebrew.

### Daniel

Daniel's Greek form has three passages the Hebrew and Aramaic lack: the prayer of Azariah and the song of the three young
men in the furnace (chapter 3), the story of Susanna (chapter 13), and Bel and the dragon (chapter 14). Catholic Bibles
include them, so their chapter 3 runs to a hundred verses; Protestant Bibles leave them out or set them apart. Compare
<ReaderLink edition="NABRE" book={34} chapter={3} verse={24} with={["LUZZI"]}>Daniel 3 in NABRE and the
Riveduta</ReaderLink>.

### Jeremiah

The Greek Jeremiah is shorter than the Hebrew (by an eighth, in the older estimate; by a sixth, in a recent one) and
puts the oracles against the nations in the middle of the book, after 25:13, rather than at the end (chapters 46–51 of
the Hebrew). Hebrew fragments found at Qumran have the shorter form too, and most scholars conclude that the
Greek translators worked from a shorter Hebrew text (see [the Qumran scrolls](/docs/qumran)). Every edition on this site follows the longer Hebrew form.

<H2 id="missing-verses">A verse one edition has and another lacks</H2>

A gap in a comparison means that the other edition has no verse with that number. Usually the words are there, under
another number: in <ReaderLink edition="NABRE" book={46} chapter={3} with={["VGCL"]}>Malachi 3 in NABRE beside the
Clementine Vulgate</ReaderLink>, NABRE's verses 19–24 are the Vulgate's chapter 4. Sometimes an edition divides a passage
into fewer verses: NABRE's Psalm 2 ends at verse 11, where others have 12, because its translators read the last lines
as one verse.

And sometimes the words are missing: a verse that late manuscripts have and the oldest do not. Modern editions,
translated from critical texts, print such verses in a note or in brackets, or leave the number out, while the Vulgate
and the Douay–Rheims keep them. *El Libro del Pueblo de Dios*, for instance, has no Mark 9:44, which
<ReaderLink edition="DRB" book={48} chapter={9} verse={44} with={["BLPD"]}>the Douay–Rheims prints</ReaderLink>. The page
on [the New Testament's text](/docs/new-testament-text) explains how editors decide.

<H2 id="printed-elsewhere">A verse printed in another chapter</H2>

An edition sometimes prints a verse where it reads best, under its own number. NABRE prints Wisdom 12:1 at the end of
<ReaderLink edition="NABRE" book={27} chapter={11}>Wisdom 11</ReaderLink>, prints the first words of Job 10:1 at the end of
Job 9, and opens <ReaderLink edition="NABRE" book={50} chapter={8}>John 8</ReaderLink> with John 7:53. The reader shows
these verses where the edition prints them, with their chapter in the number (12:1), and finds them under their own
number when you look them up.

## Sirach's prologue

The Book of Sirach opens with a prologue by its Greek translator, the author's grandson, who explains that he came to
Egypt in the thirty-eighth year of King Euergetes (132 BC) and translated his grandfather's Hebrew book there. It is part of the book in the Greek and in Catholic Bibles, but it has no verse
numbers, so editions print it differently: some before chapter 1, unnumbered; the Clementine Vulgate within its first verse;
some not at all. The reader shows it as
the book's opening page where the edition has it, as in <ReaderLink edition="NABRE" book={28} chapter={0}>NABRE's
Sirach</ReaderLink>.

## Notes and introductions

NABRE and CEI 2008 come with their translators' notes, cross-references and introductions to each book. They are part of
those editions, under the same copyright as the text, and signed-in readers can read them beside the text.
