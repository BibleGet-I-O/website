# The New Testament's text

No author's own copy of a New Testament book survives. The text is known from thousands of manuscripts copied by hand, and from
the early translations and the quotations of the Fathers. Comparing them is the work of textual criticism.

## The manuscripts

The Institute for New Testament Textual Research (INTF) in Münster keeps the official list of Greek New Testament
manuscripts. As of 29 September 2023 it listed:

| Kind | Catalogued |
|---|---|
| Papyri | 135 |
| Majuscules (written in capitals, on parchment) | 283 |
| Minuscules (written in a cursive hand) | 2,860 |
| Lectionaries (the readings arranged for the liturgy) | 2,422 |
| **All Greek witnesses** | **5,700** |

These count entries in the list, not objects: leaving out the manuscripts lost or destroyed since they were catalogued
leaves 5,541. Among the majuscules are the great codices of
the fourth and fifth centuries: [Sinaiticus](/docs/codex-sinaiticus), [Vaticanus](/docs/codex-vaticanus) and
[Alexandrinus](/docs/codex-alexandrinus).

## How a critical edition is made

The manuscripts differ, mostly in small ways (spelling, word order, a word added or left out), sometimes in whole
passages. A critical edition compares them and prints, for each place, the reading its editors judge to be the oldest,
with an apparatus that records the others and the witnesses for each. In the words of the Pontifical Biblical
Commission, textual criticism "seeks, according to certain rules, to establish a biblical text as close as possible to
the original text".

The edition most used today is the *Novum Testamentum Graece* of Nestle and Aland, whose 28th edition appeared in 2012;
it added newly published papyri to its apparatus and changed the text of the Catholic Letters in more than thirty places.
The United Bible Societies' *Greek New Testament*, made for translators, had its fifth edition in 2014, and a sixth has
followed. Modern translations are made from such critical texts; the Vulgate, and the Douay–Rheims translated from it,
keep readings that the critical editions set aside.

## Two well-known passages

**The ending of Mark.** The Gospel of Mark seems to end abruptly at 16:8, with the women fleeing the empty tomb in fear.
The longer ending (16:9–20) is not in Codex Vaticanus, and a shorter ending is found in four Greek manuscripts of the
seventh to ninth centuries (and, standing alone, in one Old Latin witness). NABRE's
note explains that the longer ending "has traditionally been accepted as a canonical part of the gospel and was defined
as such by the Council of Trent", though its vocabulary and style point to an author other than Mark; it existed by the
second century, as the Fathers' quotations show.

**The woman caught in adultery (John 7:53–8:11).** The passage is missing from all early Greek manuscripts, and others
place it elsewhere: after John 7:36, at the end of John, or in Luke. NABRE's note adds that "the Catholic Church accepts
this passage as canonical scripture". NABRE prints it in its place, and opens chapter 8 with John 7:53 (see [why
editions differ](/docs/why-editions-differ#printed-elsewhere)). Codex Alexandrinus cannot tell us either way: the two
leaves that held John 6:50–8:52 are lost.
