# The Aleppo Codex

The Aleppo Codex is a Hebrew Bible written at Tiberias, in Galilee, in the tenth century, and given its vowels, accents
and Masoretic notes by Aharon ben Asher, the most renowned of the Masoretes. It was long held to be the most exact copy
of the [Hebrew text](/docs/hebrew-text), and Maimonides took it as his model. Its Hebrew name is *Keter Aram Tzova*,
"the Crown of Aleppo", after the city where it was kept for some six hundred years. About two fifths of it were lost in
1947.

## A Bible by Ben Asher

The codex carries no date of its own. Its scribe, Shlomo ben Buya'a, also wrote a Pentateuch now in Saint Petersburg
whose inscriptions date it to 929, so the Aleppo Codex is dated to about the same time: "around the year 929" in the
Ben-Zvi Institute's nomination to UNESCO, "930 approximately" on its current page.

A dedication added about a century later, and now lost, recorded how it was made: "the complete codex of the
twenty-four books, written by our teacher and rabbi Shlomo known as Ben Boya'a, the swift scribe", and "vocalized and
transmitted with great meticulousness" by "Rabbi Aharon the son of master Rabbi Asher". Shlomo wrote the consonants;
Ben Asher added the vowels, the cantillation marks and the notes.

## Jerusalem and Cairo

The same dedication says that Yisrael ben Simha of Basra gave the codex to the Karaite community of Jerusalem, in the
care of two of its leaders, to be read at the three pilgrimage feasts, and that any Jew, Karaite or Rabbanite, could come
and consult it. After the Crusaders took Jerusalem in 1099 the codex was ransomed and passed to the Jews of Egypt; another
lost inscription recorded its transfer, by "the law of redemption from imprisonment", to the congregation of the
Jerusalemites in Fustat, old Cairo.

## Maimonides' model

In Egypt Maimonides used it. In his code of Jewish law, the *Mishneh Torah*, he wrote of the rules for writing a Torah
scroll:

> The copy on which I relied is the well-known Egyptian codex which contains the twenty-four books of the Scriptures
> and which had been in Jerusalem for several years, used as the standard text for the examination of scrolls. Everyone
> relied upon it because it had been examined by Ben Asher who closely studied it for many years and examined it again
> whenever it was being copied. This codex was the text on which I relied in the scroll of the Law that I wrote
> according to the rules. (*Hilkhot Sefer Torah* 8:4, trans. Moses Hyamson)

He relied on it for the open and closed sections of the Torah and the layout of its songs. That his "Egyptian codex" is
the Aleppo Codex was once denied, but scholars have since shown it: the Song of Moses (Deuteronomy 32) is written in the
codex in 67 lines, the number the oldest manuscripts of the *Mishneh Torah* give, and his list of the words that open
its lines matches the codex.

## Six centuries in Aleppo

How and when the codex reached Aleppo, in Syria, is not known for certain. The nomination to UNESCO says it was brought
there "apparently" by Rabbi David ben Yehoshua, a descendant of Maimonides, in 1375; the Ben-Zvi Institute's own studies
call this a possibility, and the first clear evidence of it in Aleppo is from the fifteenth century. The Jews of Aleppo
kept it in the city's great synagogue, in an iron chest with two keys held by two men, so that it could be opened only
when both were present, and called it the Crown.

## 1947

On 1 December 1947 rioters broke into the great synagogue of Aleppo (the Institute's older account gives 2 December).
The chest was broken open and the codex thrown down and scattered. It was first thought lost; in
fact it had been rescued and hidden. Sources differ on whether fire touched it: the synagogue was "sacked and burnt", but
laboratory study of the codex found its damage due to fungi, not fire.

In 1958 the codex was smuggled out of Syria, through Turkey, and given to the President of Israel, Izhak Ben-Zvi. In 1962
the rabbinical court of Jerusalem appointed a committee of trustees to own it. It was restored at the Israel Museum from
1986, and since 1993 it has been kept, on permanent loan, in the Shrine of the Book, beside the Dead Sea Scrolls.

## What was lost

When it reached Israel the codex had 294 leaves, written on both sides, out of about 487 (the Institute's older studies
count 295 of 491). Almost all of the Pentateuch is gone, up to Deuteronomy 28:17, and so are the end of the Writings, from
Song of Songs 3:11 (Ecclesiastes, Lamentations, Esther, Daniel and Ezra–Nehemiah), with smaller gaps in Kings, Jeremiah,
the Twelve Prophets and the Psalms. A few pieces have come back: a whole leaf of 2 Chronicles in 1982, and a fragment of
Exodus 8, found in Brooklyn in 1988. Old photographs preserve three lost pages of the Pentateuch.

The lost parts are reconstructed from manuscripts close to it, above all the [Leningrad Codex](/docs/codex-leningrad),
whose vowels and accents are very similar.

## The codex today

The Hebrew University Bible Project takes the Aleppo Codex as the base text of its critical edition, and published a
facsimile of it in 1976; the *Jerusalem Crown* Bible (2000) prints a text established from it and the manuscripts close
to it. In 2015 UNESCO inscribed it in its Memory of the World register. The Ben-Zvi Institute is preparing a new digital
edition, leaf by leaf, at [aleppocodex.org](http://www.aleppocodex.org/).
