# How the Bible reached us

No one owns the Bible's original pages. What we have are copies, and copies of copies, made by hand for more than two
thousand years, then translations, then printed editions. Each Bible on this site stands at the end of that chain. This
page follows it in a few steps; each step has a page of its own for those who want more.

## The Hebrew scriptures

The books of the Old Testament were written over many centuries, mostly in Hebrew, with some chapters in Aramaic. The
Jewish tradition arranges them in three parts: the Law (*Torah*), the Prophets (*Nevi'im*) and the Writings
(*Ketuvim*). Scribes copied them with great care, and in the early Middle Ages the scholars known as the Masoretes, at
Tiberias in Galilee, added signs for the vowels and the chanting, and notes to guard the text. The oldest
complete Hebrew Bible that survives is their work, the [Leningrad Codex](/docs/codex-leningrad) of 1008; the older
[Aleppo Codex](/docs/codex-aleppo), of the tenth century, has lost about two fifths of its leaves. See
[the Hebrew text](/docs/hebrew-text).

## The Qumran scrolls

In 1947 Bedouin found the first of the scrolls hidden in caves near Qumran, by the Dead Sea; by 1956 eleven caves had
given up the remains of more than nine hundred manuscripts, about 230 of them books of the Bible. They were copied
between the third century BC and the first century AD, a thousand years before the Masoretes, and they show both how
faithfully the Hebrew text was handed down and that it once circulated in more than one form. See [the Qumran
scrolls](/docs/qumran).

## The Greek Bible of the early Church

From the third century BC the Jews of Alexandria translated their scriptures into Greek: first the Law, then the other
books. This translation, the Septuagint, was the Bible of the apostles' Greek-speaking hearers and of the early Church.
It includes books and passages not found in the Hebrew Bible, such as Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch and the
books of the Maccabees, with parts of Esther and Daniel. See [the Septuagint](/docs/septuagint).

## The New Testament

The New Testament has come down to us in Greek. Its text has come down in thousands of Greek manuscripts (about
5,700 are catalogued, from papyrus fragments to the books of the Middle Ages) and in early translations. See [the New Testament's text](/docs/new-testament-text).

## The great codices

From the fourth century whole Bibles were written in single volumes, or codices. Three of them, in Greek, are among the most important witnesses to the text: [Codex
Sinaiticus](/docs/codex-sinaiticus) and [Codex Vaticanus](/docs/codex-vaticanus), of the fourth century, and [Codex
Alexandrinus](/docs/codex-alexandrinus), of the fifth.

## Jerome's Latin

In the West the Church read the Bible in Latin, in translations made from the Greek. From 382, encouraged by Pope
Damasus, Saint Jerome revised the Latin Gospels and then translated most of the Old Testament from the Hebrew. His work,
the Vulgate, became the Bible of the Latin Church for more than a thousand years. The Council of Trent declared it
authentic in 1546; the Clementine edition of 1592 fixed its text, and the Nova Vulgata of 1979 revised it against the
Hebrew and Greek. See [the Vulgate](/docs/vulgate).

## The canon

The Catholic Church's Bible has 73 books: 46 in the Old Testament and 27 in the New. The Council of Trent, in 1546,
listed them, following the Council of Florence (1442). The Old Testament's seven deuterocanonical books (Tobit, Judith,
1 and 2 Maccabees, Wisdom, Sirach and Baruch) and the Greek parts of Esther and Daniel come from the Greek Bible. The
churches of the Reformation kept only the books of the Hebrew Bible, 39, and so their Bibles have 66. The Second Vatican
Council taught that the books of both Testaments, "in their entirety, with all their parts", are sacred and canonical
(*Dei Verbum*, 11).

## Printing and the editions on this site

Printing changed how the Bible was handed on: the Gutenberg Bible, a Vulgate, was finished at Mainz around 1455; the
first complete printed Hebrew Bible came out at Soncino, near Cremona, on 22 April 1488; Erasmus published the first
Greek New Testament in 1516.
Modern scholars compare the manuscripts to establish critical editions of the Hebrew, Greek and Latin texts, and the
Church asks that translations be made "especially from the original texts" (*Dei Verbum*, 22).

The editions you can read here come from different points in this story. The Clementine Vulgate is Jerome's Latin as
fixed in 1592, and the Douay–Rheims is an English translation of the Vulgate; the Nova Vulgata is the Church's revised
Latin; NABRE, CEI 2008, *El Libro del Pueblo de Dios* and the Riveduta are modern translations (the pages on each
edition, to come, will say what each was translated from).
That is why they do not always agree: see [why editions differ](/docs/why-editions-differ).
